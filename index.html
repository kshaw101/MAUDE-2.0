The project

Can reporting patterns help improve communication?

The main question is: How can patterns in medical-device adverse-event reports help improve how patients and providers communicate problems and feedback to medical-device companies?

Who this is for

Clinicians, patients, and medical-device design teams who want a clearer way to explore the kinds of problems reported to the FDA.

What the data can tell us

The summaries show where reported device-record volume is concentrated and how selected device categories make up each company's yearly records. They do not measure the risk of using a device.

Source: U.S. FDA MAUDE / Medical Device Reporting (MDR) data files. The supplied summary files cover ten selected companies and year labels 2021–2025. The exact year/date basis and full-year coverage were not independently verified.

Device categories

The infusion-pump label has the largest total in this dataset: 1,306,749 records. Exact labels are kept separate; similarly named categories are not merged.

Why normalization helps: the trend chart expresses a device category as a percentage of that same company's total device records for that year. This makes company mix easier to compare when raw record volumes differ.

It does not adjust for sales, units in use, or patient exposure, and it is not a device failure rate.

Selected trends as a share of each company's yearly records

Each point is 100 × category records ÷ all device records for that company and year. Hover or focus on a point to see its numerator and denominator. Missing category/year rows are shown as gaps, not assumed zeros.

Sensitivity check: infusion pumps remain the leading category when considering all ten companies or the five largest by record volume. When BD is excluded, automated insulin-dosing systems become the leading category. The leading result therefore depends partly on which companies are included.

Together, the examples suggest that reporting forms should make it easy to record device identity, what happened, context of use, and immediate care effects—while leaving room to say when harm was not reported. The sample cannot establish how common any theme is.

Conclusion

Make reports easier to compare, while preserving their context

The dataset supports a focused next step: test whether a clearer, structured reporting path helps providers communicate device problems with enough context for follow-up. It does not show that a particular company or device is less safe, nor whether companies respond faster.

What I can conclude

Reported-record volume is concentrated in a few company/category groups. Comparing within-company shares offers a more balanced view of category mix than comparing raw company totals alone.

What remains uncertain

These are not unique patient or event counts. The data lacks exposure denominators, verified date coverage, and a documented sampling method for narrative excerpts. Reporting volume cannot be used as a safety ranking.

Proposed future experiment · not run

Would a guided form make useful reports easier to submit?

The proposed change is a reporting path that helps providers identify a product/model and describe what happened, use context, and immediate care effects.

A · Baseline

Current reporting route

Participants use the existing product information and reporting/contact route.

B · Proposed change

Guided reporting route

Participants use a guided product catalog and structured prompts for device identity, event description, and care impact.

Participants and assignment: Recruit provider teams from practices or hospitals using the participating company's devices; randomly assign sites to A or B and run both versions during the same period.
Proposed primary measure: the share of providers who submit a report with the device/model, what happened, and care impact recorded during a standardized reporting task. Define the task window and success threshold before recruitment.
Watch for harm: incomplete or inaccurate reports, duplicate reports, or reports that incorrectly attribute user error to a device could make the process worse even if more reports are submitted.
Interpretation: more complete reporting would support further testing; it would not by itself prove faster company action or improved patient outcomes.

This is a proposed usability experiment, not a result from the MAUDE dataset. Users with limited internet access or lower comfort with websites may be underrepresented.

Sources and method

Data notes

  • FDA MAUDE / MDR data files — source referenced by the project notebook.
  • Unit shown in the charts: device records. These summaries cannot recover unique reports, people, or incidents.
  • Company and device charts use the ten-company CSV extracts supplied for the project; similar category labels are not automatically merged.
  • The normalized share is the category's portion of its company-year total, including unspecified category labels in the denominator. It is useful for comparing composition, but not exposure-adjusted.
  • The narrative summary uses 30 supplied excerpts and the notebook's keyword lists. Counts can overlap; manual review and a documented sample design would be needed for stronger qualitative claims.

This page is a portfolio summary of an exploratory student project. MAUDE reports are signals submitted to the FDA and should not be interpreted here as proof of causation or comparative device safety.
